## Executive Summary

**The literature does not answer the question directly, and the most important result of this research is knowing precisely why.** Ports-and-adapters gives **no objective criterion** for when a boundary deserves a port — Cockburn calls it *"largely a matter of taste"* with *"no particular damage in choosing the 'wrong' number"* (Finding 1.1). The mutation-testing field is **silent** on whether production code may be changed to make a mutant killable, because the entire field holds the program fixed and varies the test suite (Finding 3.3, Gap G1). Any DESIGN artifact claiming external authority for or against extracting this port would be over-claiming. The decision must be made on this repo's own rules plus a security-specific cost/benefit — which is exactly what the evidence *does* support.

**Three converging lines of evidence make the case for adding the seam, and they are stronger than expected.** First, mutation score is an empirically validated proxy for real-fault detection — Just et al. found a coupling effect for **73% of 357 real faults** and a correlation *stronger than statement coverage's* (Finding 3.1), so a missed mutant is not a metric artefact. Second, Google's own `productive` / `unproductive` taxonomy — the framework that lets them suppress 85% of mutants as noise — classifies this particular mutant as **productive**: it is not trivially equivalent, and a test for it asserts the *specification* ("install failure ⇒ alloc `Failed`"), not the implementation, which is the opposite of the change-detector archetype they name as unproductive (Finding 3.2). The current `exclude_re` entry is therefore suppressing a mutant the literature's own taxonomy says should be killed. Third, and decisively, this is **security-critical error-handling code**: Yuan et al. (OSDI '14) found *"the majority of catastrophic failures could easily have been prevented by performing simple testing on error handling code – the last line of defense"*, and Saltzer & Schroeder's fail-safe-defaults rationale warns that a broken *exclusion* mechanism *"tends to fail by allowing access, a failure which may go unnoticed in normal use"* (Findings 5.1, 5.2). A no-op'd `fail_closed_on_mtls_install` leaves a workload **Running with no mTLS interception** and nothing alarming. That asymmetry justifies a seam cost an ordinary error path would not.

**The recommendation is Candidate A — extract an `MtlsIntercept` port trait as a 4th mandatory `Arc<dyn …>` on `MtlsInterceptWorker::new`.** It is the only candidate that satisfies every named repo rule simultaneously, and critically it is the only one that does **not** violate *"production code is not shaped by simulation"*: production gains one more port indistinguishable in shape from the three the worker already holds, with no fault field, no `cfg`, no optional seam. Candidate **C** (`#[cfg(feature)]` fault field) is rejected on three independent grounds, the first of which is fatal on its own — feature-gated code is invisible to the **default lane**, which is precisely what #250 requires (Finding 4.1). Candidate **B** abstracts the wrong thing: the un-ownable surface is `libc`/`nft`, not `MtlsInterceptWorker`. Kubernetes provides the closest transferable precedent, having converged on a **domain-level fake** of `iptables` in preference to injected-`exec` mocking (Finding 6.1). The honest counter-evidence is Cilium, the nearest peer project, which chose *not* to abstract its kernel surface and instead gates privileged tests behind `//go:build privileged_tests` — but that approach can only exercise privileged code **for success**; it offers no way to make a privileged call fail on demand, which is the whole requirement (Finding 6.2, Conflict 2). Nine open questions — port granularity, sync-vs-async signature, owning crate, Earned-Trust `probe()`, the real `#[non_exhaustive]` construction blocker, and the assertion surface — are left explicitly for the architect to pin.

## Findings

### RQ1 — Ports & Adapters: when does a boundary deserve a port?

#### Finding 1.1: Cockburn provides NO objective criterion for port granularity — it is explicitly a matter of taste

**Evidence**: Cockburn's original ports-and-adapters write-up states that **"what exactly a port is and isn't is largely a matter of taste"**, and that **"it doesn't appear that there is any particular damage in choosing the 'wrong' number of ports, so that remains a matter of intuition."** He notes **"Many applications have only two ports: the user-side dialog and the database-side dialog"**, while his own preference **"tends to favor a small number, two, three or four ports."**
**Source**: [Alistair Cockburn, "Hexagonal Architecture"](https://alistair.cockburn.us/hexagonal-architecture/) — Accessed 2026-08-01. Domain: `alistair.cockburn.us` (primary/originator source; not on the supplied trusted-domain list but is the *canonical primary source* for the pattern — treated as authoritative-by-authorship, flagged below).
**Confidence**: Medium-High (single primary source; corroborated indirectly by Fowler below)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: This is a **materially important negative result** for RQ1. The research prompt asked for "the stated criterion for a boundary deserving a port." **There is no such stated criterion in the originating material.** Anyone arguing "the literature says extract a port here" or "the literature says this is over-abstraction" is over-claiming. The decision for #250 therefore cannot be settled by appeal to hexagonal-architecture authority; it must be settled on *this repo's own* stated rules plus the cost/benefit evidence below.

#### Finding 1.2: The originating material DOES name test-drivability as a first-class motivation for ports

**Evidence**: Cockburn's stated benefit includes that **"the test or QA department can run automated test scripts against the application"** without depending on the UI, and that adapters are interchangeable such that **"the conversation across the API should not change"** when the backing technology changes.
**Source**: [Alistair Cockburn, "Hexagonal Architecture"](https://alistair.cockburn.us/hexagonal-architecture/) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: Corroborated by the "don't mock what you don't own" adapter prescription (Finding 1.3) and by this repo's own existing precedent (`SimDriver`, `SimMtlsResolve`, `SimObservationStore`), which is the codebase's operative reading of the same pattern.
**Confidence**: High
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Replacing a real actor (here: a privileged kernel/`nft` surface requiring `CAP_NET_ADMIN`) with a test-controllable adapter is a *sanctioned, original-intent* use of a port — not a testing-only hack bolted on. This distinguishes candidate **A** from candidate **C** in kind, not merely in degree.

#### Finding 1.3: "Don't mock what you don't own" prescribes exactly the wrapper/adapter shape as the remedy for un-fakeable third-party surfaces

**Evidence**: The canonical statement of the heuristic prescribes: **"Introduce your own shim/wrapper/adapter around it. This effectively cordons off the dependency to a single place in your codebase and contextualizes it in the consistent and easy-to-use style you're trying to promote within your codebase."** Stated benefits: the wrapper **"serves as a specification of what you're using in that dependency,"** making replacement easier because **"you know it only needs to do those three things (as opposed to dozens)."** The failure mode without a wrapper is named explicitly: if faking the third-party surface is *painless* it **"may leak third-party dependencies into your application, mixing levels of abstraction"**; if faking is *painful*, the pain buys nothing because **"the design feedback of specifying the interaction wasn't actionable."**
**Source**: [testdouble/contributing-tests wiki, "Don't mock what you don't own"](https://github.com/testdouble/contributing-tests/wiki/Don't-mock-what-you-don't-own) — Domain: `github.com` (trusted, industry_leaders, 0.8) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: [Google Testing Blog, "Testing on the Toilet: Don't Mock Types You Don't Own"](https://testing.googleblog.com/2020/07/testing-on-toilet-dont-mock-types-you.html) (accessed 2026-08-01) states the same remedy — wrap the third-party type in a project-owned interface and mock that; and [Fowler, "Mocks Aren't Stubs"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/mocksArentStubs.html) supplies the underlying rationale (mock-based tests are "more coupled to the implementation").
**Confidence**: High (3 independent sources: testdouble, Google Testing Blog, Fowler)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: This is the single most *directly on-point* piece of guidance for #250. `libc::socket` + `setsockopt(IP_TRANSPARENT)`, the `nft`/`ip` subprocess surface, and `TcpListener::local_addr` are all **types/APIs we do not own**. The heuristic's prescription is precisely candidate **A**: own a thin interface over the un-ownable surface, and fake *that*. It also warns against the *painless-fake* failure mode, which is what a `#[cfg(feature)]` boolean fault flag (candidate C) is — a fake so painless it teaches nothing about the boundary and leaks the test's existence into production types.

#### Finding 1.4: Fowler's own position is skeptical of mock-heavy isolation but does not forbid the adapter

**Evidence**: Fowler distinguishes **classical TDD** ("use real objects if possible and a double if it's awkward to use the real thing") from **mockist TDD** ("will always use a mock for any object with interesting behavior"). He warns **"Mockist tests are thus more coupled to the implementation of a method. Changing the nature of calls to collaborators usually cause a mockist test to break,"** and concludes he does not **"see any compelling benefits for mockist TDD, and am concerned about the consequences of coupling tests to implementation."**
**Source**: [Martin Fowler, "Mocks Aren't Stubs"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/mocksArentStubs.html) — Domain: `martinfowler.com` (trusted, industry_leaders, 0.8) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Confidence**: High
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Note the **classicist criterion is satisfied here**: "use a double if it's *awkward to use the real thing*." The real thing requires `CAP_NET_ADMIN`, a real `nft` binary, and Linux — i.e. it is out of the default lane by this repo's own `.claude/rules/testing.md` definition. That is squarely "awkward." Fowler's concern (implementation coupling) applies to *behaviour-verifying mocks*; the repo's existing precedent (`SimObservationStore::inject_write_failure`, `SimDriver::fail_on_start_with`) are **fakes/stubs with fault scripting**, verified by *state* (the resulting `AllocStatusRow`), not by call expectations — so Fowler's objection largely does not bite.

### RQ2 — Testability as a design force: legitimate driver or design damage?

#### Finding 2.1: The strongest case AGAINST (DHH) — the criterion is "would you call this better if you deleted the tests?"

**Evidence**: DHH names as damage code **"warped out of shape solely to accommodate testing objectives"**, specifically citing hexagonal-style layering where **"the simple controller is forbidden from talking directly to Active Record, now it has to go through the Repository. And the action itself is hollowed out to extract a Command object."** His falsifiable criterion: **"If you did not look or care about the tests, you wouldn't look at the 'after code' and say 'BETTER!'"** He objects to **"needless indirection and conceptual overhead"** even when it enables faster or more isolated tests, and proposes integration/system tests instead of isolation-driven indirection: **"Test controllers using integration tests. Don't even try to make it fit under the unit testing paradigm."** His slogan: **"You do not let your tests drive your design, you let your design drive your tests!"**
**Source**: [DHH, "Test-induced design damage"](https://dhh.dk/2014/test-induced-design-damage.html) — Domain: `dhh.dk` (NOT on the supplied trusted-domain list; cited as the **primary source for the position itself**, which is the only reason it appears here — see Source Analysis caveat).
**Verification**: [Martin Fowler, "Is TDD Dead?"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/is-tdd-dead/) (trusted, 0.8) records the same claim: **"every layer of indirection introduces a high cost."** [Thoughtworks, "Test-Induced Design Damage. Fallacy or Reality?"](https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/blog/test-induced-design-damage-fallacy-or-reality) independently attributes the concept and locates the mechanism: *"One of the principle causes of test induced design damage is confusion and complication that comes from excessive indirection."*
**Confidence**: High (3 sources, one primary + two independent secondary)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Applied to #250, DHH's criterion asks: **would a `MtlsIntercept` port be an improvement if the tests did not exist?** A defensible "yes" exists — it names an implicit boundary (the privileged Linux install surface) that is currently a scattering of free functions, and it is the boundary the whole crate already models three times over (`MtlsEnforcement`, `MtlsResolve`, `Clock`). But this is exactly the question DESIGN must answer honestly rather than assume.

#### Finding 2.2: The strongest case FOR — Beck and Fowler reject the causal link and reframe it as a per-decision trade-off

**Evidence**: Beck rejected the causal link, stating the issue was **"one design decision at a time,"** not inherent to TDD, comparing the blame to **"driving a car to a bad place and blaming the car for it."** Fowler argued the real driver was not TDD but **"a desire for isolation."** The debate's synthesised criteria were: (1) **lifespan/context** of the code, (2) actual changeability — **"If I screw up this line of code is a test going to fail?"**, and (3) explicit trade-off analysis — **"How much are we willing to spend to get how much decoupling?"** The series explicitly reports that **no universal formula emerged.**
**Source**: [Martin Fowler, "Is TDD Dead?"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/is-tdd-dead/) — Domain: `martinfowler.com` (trusted, 0.8) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: [Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/blog/test-induced-design-damage-fallacy-or-reality) records the same three positions and the same lack of a universal formula.
**Confidence**: Medium-High (2 independent sources; the primary artifact is a video series, so both are summaries)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Criterion (2) is the decisive one for #250 and it answers itself: **"If I screw up this line of code is a test going to fail?"** — for `fail_closed_on_mtls_install`, the mutation result proves the answer is **no**. This is the *strongest single argument in the literature* for spending design budget here, and it is Beck-and-Fowler-sanctioned rather than a testing-tail-wagging-dog move. Criterion (3) is what makes candidate **B** (abstracting one level up, at the action_shim) look expensive relative to its payoff.

### RQ3 — Mutation testing literature: validity, equivalent mutants, and changing production code

#### Finding 3.1: Mutation score IS an empirically validated proxy for real-fault detection — and a *stronger* one than statement coverage

**Evidence**: Just et al. studied **357 real faults** across **5 open-source Java programs (321 KLOC)** and **230,000 mutants**, using both developer-written and automatically generated test suites, and explicitly controlling for code coverage as a confound. Their stated contributions: *"An investigation of the coupling effect between real faults and the mutants that are generated by commonly used mutation operators. The results show the existence of a coupling effect for **73% of real faults**."* And: *"An analysis of whether mutant detection is correlated with real fault detection. The results show a statistically significant correlation that is **stronger than the correlation between statement coverage and real fault detection**."* The abstract adds: *"The results show a statistically significant correlation between mutant detection and real fault detection, **independently of code coverage**."*
**Source**: [Just, Jalali, Inozemtseva, Ernst, Holmes, Fraser, "Are Mutants a Valid Substitute for Real Faults in Software Testing?", FSE 2014](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rtholmes/papers/fse_2014_just.pdf) — Domain: `cs.ubc.ca` (academic, high, 1.0). Also in [ACM DL 10.1145/2635868.2635929](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2635868.2635929) (`dl.acm.org`, trusted academic, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01. Read from the primary PDF, pages 1–2.
**Verification**: [Petrović, Ivanković, Fraser, Just, "Practical Mutation Testing at Scale: A View from Google", arXiv:2102.11378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11378) (`arxiv.org`, academic, 1.0) independently states *"Code coverage might be misleading, in particular when program statements are covered but the expected program outcome is not asserted upon… Mutation analysis addresses the limitations of code coverage and is widely considered the best approach for evaluating test suite efficacy."* Jia & Harman's IEEE TSE survey is the standard third anchor for the field's validity claims.
**Confidence**: High (3 independent sources, all high-reputation academic)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: This settles RQ3(a) **affirmatively** and, importantly, settles the *counter*-argument to #250 that "a missed mutant is just a metric artifact." It is not: mutant detection is a validated proxy, and it is a *better* proxy than the coverage number this handler already satisfies. Note also the paper's own honest limit — **17% of real faults are not coupled to any mutant** and **10% require a stronger operator** — so a *killed* mutant is not proof of correctness. That asymmetry matters: killing this mutant is necessary, not sufficient.

#### Finding 3.2: The accepted practice for unkillable mutants is a *taxonomy* — `equivalent` vs `unproductive` vs `productive` — and the taxonomy classifies the #250 mutant as PRODUCTIVE

**Evidence**: Google's definition: *"An **unproductive** mutant is either trivially equivalent to the original program **or** it is detectable, but adding a test for it would not improve the test suite. For example, mutating the initial capacity of a Java collection (e.g., `new ArrayList(64)` → `new ArrayList(16)`) creates an unproductive mutant. While it is possible to write a test that asserts on the collection capacity or expected memory allocations, **it is unproductive to do so**. In fact, it is conceivable that these tests, if written and added, would even have a negative impact because their **change-detector nature (specifically testing the current implementation rather than the specification) violates testing best practices and causes brittle tests and false alarms.**"* Google reports that *"developers at Google initially classified **85% of reported mutants as unproductive**"*, and that their suppression heuristics *"improved the ratio of productive mutants from **15% to 89%**."* Their `arid node` mechanism is exactly this suppression: *"An AST node is eligible for mutation if it is covered by at least one test **and if it is not arid** (i.e., if mutated, it does not create unproductive mutants)."*
Separately, the classical *equivalent mutant* problem is defined as mutants that *"can never be killed because they always produce the same output as the original program… syntactically different but functionally equivalent,"* and *"automatically detecting all equivalent mutants is impossible because program equivalence is undecidable."*
**Source**: [Petrović, Ivanković, Fraser, Just, "Practical Mutation Testing at Scale: A View from Google", arXiv:2102.11378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11378) — Domain: `arxiv.org` (academic, high, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01, read from primary PDF pages 1–3. Equivalent-mutant definition per [Jia & Harman, "An Analysis and Survey of the Development of Mutation Testing", IEEE TSE 37(5):649–678, 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TSE.2010.62) (`ieee.org`/`dl.acm.org`, academic, 1.0).
**Verification**: [Petrović & Ivanković, "State of Mutation Testing at Google", ICSE-SEIP 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3183519.3183521) (`dl.acm.org`, 1.0) documents the same arid-line suppression approach independently of the later arXiv paper.
**Confidence**: High (3 sources)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: This is the decisive framing for #250. Apply Google's own two-pronged unproductive test to the `fail_closed_on_mtls_install → Ok(())` mutant:
1. *Trivially equivalent to the original?* **No** — it changes observable state (no `Failed` `AllocStatusRow`, no `MtlsInterceptInstallFailed` reason, no driver stop, gate handling differs).
2. *Detectable but a test for it wouldn't improve the suite?* **No** — a test asserting "when mTLS install fails, the alloc is driven to `Failed` with reason `MtlsInterceptInstallFailed`" is a **specification** assertion, not a change-detector. It is the exact opposite of the `ArrayList(64)` example Google names as the archetype of an unproductive mutant.
Therefore the current `.cargo/mutants.toml` `exclude_re` entry is, in Google's vocabulary, **suppressing a productive mutant** — a form of suppression the literature explicitly does not sanction. The suppression is legitimate only as a *temporary* marker (which the "REMOVE when #250 lands" comment correctly acknowledges).

#### Finding 3.3: The literature sanctions *test* changes for productive mutants; it is largely SILENT on production-code changes — with one instructive exception

**Evidence**: Google's entire surfacing workflow is framed around tests, not production code: surviving mutants are *"surfaced as code findings"* in code review, where *"findings do not need to be resolved by the author before submission, unless a human reviewer marks them as mandatory."* Their motivating example is a fully-covered-but-weakly-asserted function, where *"The tests only exercise the function, but do not assert upon its effects on the returned buffer… The statement-deletion mutation, on the other hand, explicitly points out this **testing weakness**."* The prescribed remedy throughout is *"create test cases"* / improve assertions.
**Source**: [Petrović et al., arXiv:2102.11378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11378) — Accessed 2026-08-01 (primary PDF, pages 1–3).
**Confidence**: Medium (this is an *absence* claim — the literature not addressing production-code seams — established from 2 sources rather than a positive statement; see Knowledge Gap G1)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: **This is a genuine gap in the literature and must be reported as such rather than papered over.** The mutation-testing literature treats the program under test as *fixed* and the test suite as the variable — the entire field is defined that way. It therefore offers **no direct sanction for, and no direct prohibition of, changing production code to make a mutant killable.** Two consequences for #250:
- Anyone claiming "the mutation-testing literature says you should refactor for killability" is over-claiming. It says no such thing.
- Anyone claiming "the literature forbids it" is equally over-claiming. The question is simply out of the field's declared scope.
The decision must therefore be made on **design** grounds (RQ1/RQ2) and **security** grounds (RQ5), with the mutation result serving only as *evidence that an untested specification-level behaviour exists* — which is exactly what Finding 3.1 licenses it to do.

#### Finding 3.4: Caveat — mutation score's correlation with real faults weakens when test-suite size is controlled

**Evidence**: Papadakis, Shin, Yoo & Bae (ICSE 2018) performed a large-scale study specifically controlling for **test suite size** as a confound, noting that *"larger test suites are more likely to detect more faults and kill more mutants,"* and re-examined whether mutation scores are a valid proxy under that control.
**Source**: [Papadakis, Shin, Yoo, Bae, "Are Mutation Scores Correlated with Real Fault Detection? A Large Scale Empirical Study on the Relationship Between Mutants and Real Faults", ICSE 2018, DOI 10.1145/3180155.3180183](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3180155.3180183) — Domain: `dl.acm.org` (academic, high, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Confidence**: **Low** — the full text returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch; the claim above is sourced from the paper's abstract/metadata and the search-result summary only. **Do not treat the strength or direction of the size-controlled result as established by this document.** See Knowledge Gap G2.
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Flagged for completeness and intellectual honesty. Even at its strongest, this caveat targets *aggregate mutation score as a suite-quality metric*, not the far weaker claim #250 actually relies on: that **one specific surviving mutant identifies one specific unasserted behaviour**. Google's own workflow (Finding 3.3) makes precisely this distinction — they abandoned computing an absolute mutation score in favour of surfacing individual actionable mutants.

### RQ4 — Rust-specific mechanics

#### RQ4.1 — `#[cfg(test)]` does not cross crate boundaries; the `cfg(any(test, feature))` idiom

**Finding 4.1a — Confirmed: `cfg(test)` is not set for the library when an integration test links it.**
**Evidence**: The Rust Book: *"The `#[cfg(test)]` annotation on the `tests` module tells Rust to compile and run the test code only when you run `cargo test`, not when you run `cargo build`."* And on integration tests: *"In Rust, integration tests are entirely external to your library. They use your library in the same way any other code would, which means they can only call functions that are part of your library's public API."* … *"Each file in the tests directory is a separate crate."* The tracked rustc issue states the consequence directly: *"The `#[cfg(test)]` attribute is **not enabled** when compiling the library crate for use by integration tests. Integration tests link against the library as an external consumer would, but they cannot access code gated behind `#[cfg(test)]` conditionals."*
**Source**: [The Rust Programming Language, Ch. 11.3 "Test Organization"](https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch11-03-test-organization.html) — Domain: `doc.rust-lang.org` (trusted open_source/official, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: [rust-lang/rust issue #84629, "cfg(test) is not set during integration tests"](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/issues/84629) (`github.com`, trusted, 0.8) — Accessed 2026-08-01; issue is open and tracked by the testing-devex team, i.e. this is *acknowledged current behaviour*, not a bug about to change. Third corroboration: [Rust Reference, conditional compilation](https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/conditional-compilation.html).
**Confidence**: High (3 sources)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: This is a **hard constraint on candidate C** and it compounds with a second constraint the prompt already notes: the target handler lives in `overdrive-control-plane` while the failing surface lives in `overdrive-worker`/`overdrive-mtls`. Any `#[cfg(test)]` seam is therefore invisible to the test that needs it, regardless of which crate the test lives in. The only `cfg`-based escape is a **Cargo feature**, which is exactly where the next hazard bites.

**Finding 4.1b — Cargo feature unification makes a fault-injection feature leak into production builds of the same crate.**
**Evidence**: Cargo docs: *"When a dependency is used by multiple packages, Cargo will use the **union of all features** enabled on that dependency when building it."* Consequently: *"features should be **additive**. That is, enabling a feature should not disable functionality, and it should usually be safe to enable any combination of features."* And: *"This can make it difficult to ensure that the default features are not enabled, especially for a dependency that appears multiple times in the dependency graph."* Partial mitigation exists: with resolver v2, *"Dev-dependencies do not activate features unless building a Cargo target that needs them (like tests or examples)."*
**Source**: [The Cargo Book, "Features"](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/features.html) — Domain: `doc.rust-lang.org` (trusted, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: The same unification semantics are the documented rationale for resolver v2 in [The Cargo Book, "Resolver"](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/resolver.html); and this repo's own `.claude/rules/testing.md` already encodes the downstream consequence (every workspace member must declare `integration-tests = []` because per-package feature scoping otherwise breaks).
**Confidence**: High
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Two distinct hazards for candidate **C**, and the second is the serious one:
1. *Leakage*: once any target in the workspace build enables `overdrive-worker/integration-tests`, the **fault field and its branch compile into that build of the crate** — including a build that also produces the production binary in the same `cargo` invocation. Resolver v2 limits this to targets that need it, which helps, but the repo *routinely* runs `--features integration-tests` workspace-wide.
2. *Non-additivity*: a fault-injection knob is, by Cargo's own definition, borderline non-additive — enabling it does not add capability, it adds a way to **change behaviour**. Cargo's guidance ("enabling a feature should not disable functionality") is aimed at exactly this smell.
Critically, this hazard is **not hypothetical for #250**: the issue explicitly wants a **default-lane** killer test, and the default lane is by definition compiled *without* `integration-tests`. A `#[cfg(feature = "integration-tests")]` seam therefore **cannot** produce a default-lane test at all — candidate C fails on its own stated requirement before the leakage argument is even reached.

#### RQ4.2 — `Arc<dyn Trait>` vs generics for injection

**Finding 4.2 — `dyn` compatibility imposes a precise, checkable set of constraints; `async fn` in a trait is the one that bites.**
**Evidence**: The Rust Reference lists the dyn-compatibility (formerly "object safety") requirements: all supertraits dyn compatible; *"`Sized` must not be a supertrait… it must not require `Self: Sized`"*; no associated constants; no associated types with generics. Dispatchable functions must *"Not have any type parameters (lifetime parameters are allowed)"*, must take a receiver of `&self` / `&mut self` / `Box<Self>` / `Rc<Self>` / `Arc<Self>` / `Pin<P>`, and must **not have an opaque return type** — explicitly: *"Not be an `async fn` (which has a hidden `Future` type)"* and *"Not have a return position `impl Trait` type."*
**Source**: [The Rust Reference, "Traits" (dyn compatibility)](https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/items/traits.html) — Domain: `doc.rust-lang.org` (trusted, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: [`async-trait` crate docs](https://docs.rs/async-trait/latest/async_trait/) (`docs.rs`, trusted, 1.0) confirms the consequence and the workaround: *"the trait `Trait` is not dyn compatible… because method `f` is `async`"*, and the macro's desugaring is *"Async fns get transformed into methods that return `Pin<Box<dyn Future + Send + 'async_trait>>` and delegate to an async block"* — i.e. **a heap allocation per call**.
**Confidence**: High (2 authoritative sources, both first-party)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Directly consequential for candidate **A**'s signature design. The three install operations (`bind_transparent`, `install_outbound`, `install_inbound`) are **blocking syscalls / subprocess spawns**, not `.await`-ed I/O. If the port is defined with **sync** methods, it is dyn compatible with zero ceremony, no `async_trait`, and no per-call `Box` allocation — and it also sidesteps this repo's `.claude/rules/development.md` rule that `async` on real work is correct *only* in a workflow body. **Recommendation input: define the port as sync.** This also mirrors the existing sync `Dataplane`/`Clock`-shaped ports rather than inventing a new async surface. Generics/monomorphisation (`W: MtlsIntercept`) would avoid dynamic dispatch entirely but would **infect every holder type with a type parameter** — including `MtlsInterceptWorker`, the `Option<&Arc<MtlsInterceptWorker>>` threaded through `action_shim`, and the composition root — which is a far larger blast radius than the ~one virtual call per allocation start that `Arc<dyn …>` costs. On a path that already spawns a subprocess (`nft`), the dispatch cost is unmeasurable.

#### RQ4.3 — Making side-effecting free functions injectable

**Finding 4.3 — Four idiomatic options exist; the literature's own heuristic ("don't mock what you don't own") selects the trait-object wrapper.**
**Evidence & options** (synthesised; each mechanism is documented in the cited first-party references, the *comparison* is this document's analysis and is labelled as such):

| Mechanism | How | Cost / hazard |
|---|---|---|
| **Trait object** (`Arc<dyn Port>`) | Wrap the free fns in a trait; host adapter calls them verbatim; sim adapter scripts faults | One vtable dispatch; requires dyn compatibility (Finding 4.2); adds a mandatory ctor param |
| **Generic parameter** (`W: Port`) | Same trait, monomorphised | Zero dispatch cost; **type parameter propagates virally** through every owner/holder; hurts `Arc<dyn>`-style storage and trait-object collections |
| **Function-pointer / closure field** (`fn(..) -> Result<..>` or `Box<dyn Fn…>`) | Struct field holding the fn; default = real fn | Smallest diff, but it is an *optional-by-construction* seam → collides head-on with this repo's anti-builder rule; no rustdoc contract surface; no equivalence-test target |
| **`#[cfg]`-swapped module** (`#[cfg(feature=…)] use fake as imp;`) | Compile-time substitution | Blocked by RQ4.1 (features leak / no default lane); no per-test control (whole-binary, not per-case) |

The "don't mock what you don't own" heuristic explicitly prescribes the wrapper form for un-ownable APIs: *"Introduce your own shim/wrapper/adapter around it… cordons off the dependency to a single place."*
**Source**: Mechanisms per [Rust Reference — Traits](https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/items/traits.html) and [The Cargo Book — Features](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/features.html) (both `doc.rust-lang.org`, 1.0). Prescription per [testdouble wiki](https://github.com/testdouble/contributing-tests/wiki/Don't-mock-what-you-don't-own) (`github.com`, 0.8) + [Google Testing Blog](https://testing.googleblog.com/2020/07/testing-on-toilet-dont-mock-types-you.html).
**Confidence**: Medium-High (mechanisms High; the ranking is interpretation, marked as such)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: The function-pointer-field option deserves explicit rejection rather than silent omission, because it is the *cheapest* option and will be proposed. It fails this repo's rule verbatim: *"Builder-pattern overrides (`with_clock`, `with_transport`) are an anti-pattern for these traits… A builder makes the dependency optional — and 'optional' means 'tests can forget.'"* A defaulted `fn` field is precisely an optional dependency wearing a different hat.

#### RQ4.4 — `#[non_exhaustive]` + private constructors: sanctioned escapes

**Finding 4.4 — Enum-level `#[non_exhaustive]` does NOT block construction; it blocks exhaustive matching. The construction blocker must be something else.**
**Evidence**: The Rust Reference: *"Non-exhaustive types cannot be constructed outside of the defining crate: Non-exhaustive **variants** (`struct` or `enum` variant) cannot be constructed with a StructExpression… [but] `enum` **instances can be constructed**."* Its own example makes this explicit:
```rust
// Can construct an instance of `Error`; new variants being introduced would
// not result in this failing to compile.
let error = Error::Message("foo".to_string());
```
Whereas variant-level non-exhaustiveness does block it: *"Cannot construct an instance of `Message::Send` or `Message::Reaction`."* On matching: *"When pattern matching on a non-exhaustive enum, matching on a variant does not contribute towards the exhaustiveness of the arms"* — a wildcard arm is required.
**Source**: [The Rust Reference, "Type system attributes" (`non_exhaustive`)](https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/attributes/type_system.html) — Domain: `doc.rust-lang.org` (trusted, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: Same semantics documented in [The Cargo Book / SemVer compatibility guidance](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/semver.html) (`doc.rust-lang.org`, 1.0), which lists adding `#[non_exhaustive]` as a breaking change precisely because of the matching (not construction) rule.
**Confidence**: High
**Analysis (interpretation) — this corrects a likely misdiagnosis in the issue framing.** The prompt states `MtlsInterceptInstallError` is *"`#[non_exhaustive]` with private constructors"* and that an out-of-crate test can only build the `Inbound(#[from] InterceptError)` variant. Per the Reference, **the enum-level `#[non_exhaustive]` is not what is stopping construction** — a public tuple variant of a `#[non_exhaustive]` enum *is* constructible cross-crate. The actual blocker must be one of: (a) per-*variant* `#[non_exhaustive]`, (b) variant fields whose *types* are private/crate-visible, or (c) struct-expression variants. **DESIGN must confirm which**, because the sanctioned escapes differ:
- If (b)/(c): add a **public, documented constructor** (`pub fn install_failed(reason: …) -> Self`) — the ordinary, SemVer-safe escape and the one this repo already mandates (*"Consistent constructors. Every error enum variant should have an associated constructor method"*).
- `#[doc(hidden)] pub fn __for_test(...)` is an available but **weaker** escape: it is public API in SemVer terms while pretending not to be, and it is a test-shaped hole in production types — the same objection that sinks candidate C, in miniature.
- A `From` bridge is the cleanest if a public lower-level error already implies the variant, which is exactly the existing `Inbound(#[from] InterceptError)` shape.
**Important**: none of these escapes actually solves #250 on its own. Being able to *construct* the error does not let a test make `start_alloc` *return* it. Constructor access is necessary for candidates A/B/C alike, not a candidate in itself.

### RQ5 — Fault injection for security-critical fail-closed paths

#### Finding 5.1: Error-handling code is empirically the highest-value untested surface in distributed systems — "the last line of defense"

**Evidence**: Yuan et al. studied **198 randomly sampled, user-reported failures** across Cassandra, HBase, HDFS, Hadoop MapReduce, and Redis. Their headline result, verbatim: *"We found that the majority of catastrophic failures could easily have been prevented by performing simple testing on **error handling code – the last line of defense** – even without an understanding of the software design."* They extracted three simple rules into a static checker (Aspirator): *"**Over 30% of the catastrophic failures would have been prevented** had Aspirator been used and the identified bugs fixed."* Running it over 9 systems *"located 143 bugs and bad practices that have been fixed or confirmed by the developers."*
**Source**: [Yuan, Luo, Zhuang, Rodrigues, Zhao, Zhang, Jain, Stumm, "Simple Testing Can Prevent Most Critical Failures: An Analysis of Production Failures in Distributed Data-Intensive Systems", OSDI '14, USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi14/technical-sessions/presentation/yuan) — Domain: `usenix.org` (academic, high, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01, read verbatim from the USENIX proceedings PDF, p. 249 (abstract).
**Verification**: [Zhang, Morin, Haller, Baudry, Monperrus, "A Chaos Engineering System for Live Analysis and Falsification of Exception-handling in the JVM", arXiv:1805.05246 (KTH/SINTEF)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.05246) (`arxiv.org`, academic, 1.0) states independently: *"**bad exception-handling is known to be the cause of up to 92% of critical failures**"* and that chaos engineering *"can help them find weaknesses in error handling and disaster recovery routines."* Third: [ACM DL record 10.5555/2685048.2685068](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/2685048.2685068).
**Confidence**: High (3 independent high-reputation academic sources)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: This is the **strongest empirical argument in the whole document for spending real design budget on #250**. `fail_closed_on_mtls_install` is *literally* error-handling code — the last line of defense on a security-critical path — and cargo-mutants has produced direct evidence that it is untested. The Yuan et al. result says this is precisely the class of code where cheap testing buys disproportionate failure prevention. Note the further sharpening: their finding is that catastrophic failures come not from exotic bugs but from error handlers that are *trivially* wrong. **The mutant under discussion (`-> Ok(())`) is exactly a trivially-wrong error handler** — it is the mutation-testing analogue of Aspirator's "empty handler" rule.

#### Finding 5.2: Fail-safe defaults are a foundational security principle, and its rationale specifically warns that failures of *exclusion* mechanisms go unnoticed

**Evidence**: Saltzer & Schroeder's fail-safe-defaults principle: *"Base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion."* Its rationale, verbatim: *"A design or implementation mistake in a mechanism that gives explicit permission tends to fail by refusing permission, a safe situation, since it will be quickly detected. On the other hand, **a design or implementation mistake in a mechanism that explicitly excludes access tends to fail by allowing access, a failure which may go unnoticed in normal use.**"* Fail-safe defaults is principle #2 of the canonical eight (economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability).
**Source**: [Saltzer & Schroeder, "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems", Proc. IEEE 63(9), 1975](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs551/saltzer/) — Domain: `cs.virginia.edu` (academic `.edu`, high, 1.0 per the tier table) — Accessed 2026-08-01. [Published 1975; concept remains current.]
**Verification**: [Wikipedia summary of the paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Protection_of_Information_in_Computer_Systems) and [arXiv:2407.05710, "Saltzer & Schroeder for 2030: Security engineering principles in a world of AI"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.05710) (`arxiv.org`, 1.0) both restate the principle and confirm its continued canonical status.
**Confidence**: High (3 sources; one is the primary 1975 text)
**Analysis (interpretation) — this is the argument that "security-critical" genuinely changes the cost/benefit.** Saltzer & Schroeder's rationale maps onto #250 with unusual precision. `fail_closed_on_mtls_install` is the mechanism that *converts an mTLS-install failure into a refusal* (alloc → `Failed`). If it silently becomes a no-op — exactly the mutant — the allocation is left **Running without mTLS interception installed**: a workload admitted to the mesh with no identity enforcement. That is a **fail-**open**, and by Saltzer & Schroeder's own words it is the failure mode which *"may go unnoticed in normal use."* Nothing else in the system alarms on it; the alloc looks healthy. So the ordinary "error paths are lower value than happy paths" heuristic **inverts here**: this error path *is* the security control. A seam whose cost would not be justified for an ordinary error path can be justified for this one, and that justification is principled rather than ad hoc.

#### Finding 5.3: Injecting faults at the syscall boundary is an established, published technique — and the difficulty of triggering a specific syscall error is a recognised research problem, not a local failure of imagination

**Evidence**: PHOEBE is *"a novel fault injection framework for system call invocation errors"* motivated *"by the essential role of system calls to analyze software behavior, and by the significant number of invocations to system calls that naturally fail in production."* Its stated purpose: *"to systematically assess the reliability of applications with respect to system call invocation errors."* The authors concede the tractability problem: *"It is known that the space of all possible error injection is large. In other words, it is potentially intractable to explore all possible error scenarios."* Their remedy is to ground injection in *realistic* errors — those *"that naturally happen in production."*
**Source**: [Zhang, Morin, Baudry, Monperrus, "Maximizing Error Injection Realism for Chaos Engineering with System Calls", arXiv:2006.04444 (IEEE TDSC)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.04444) — Domain: `arxiv.org` (academic, high, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01, read from primary PDF p.1.
**Verification**: [ChaosMachine, arXiv:1805.05246](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.05246) (same group) applies the identical philosophy one layer up, at try-catch granularity, across *"339 try-catch blocks in 212 Java classes"* in 630 kLOC of production Java. Industry corroboration: [Microsoft Engineering Fundamentals Playbook, "Fault Injection Testing"](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/fault-injection-testing/) frames fault injection as a means *"to increase coverage and validate software robustness and error handling."*
**Confidence**: Medium-High (2 high-reputation academic sources from the same research group — **not fully independent** — plus 1 industry source; independence caveat recorded in Source Analysis)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: Two takeaways for #250. (1) The `libc::socket` / `setsockopt(IP_TRANSPARENT)` / `nft`-spawn boundary is a **recognised, principled injection point**, not an arbitrary place to cut. Choosing the port boundary to coincide with the syscall boundary is what the literature actually does. (2) The literature's own realism criterion favours injecting *errors that naturally occur*: `EPERM` from a missing `CAP_NET_ADMIN`, `nft` exiting non-zero, `EADDRINUSE` on the transparent bind. A `SimMtlsIntercept` that scripts these specific, real `MtlsInterceptInstallError` variants is closer to the literature's ideal than a generic boolean "fail now" flag — a further point against candidate C's shape.

### RQ6 — Real-world precedents: privileged syscall surfaces made testable

#### Finding 6.1: Kubernetes migrated AWAY from injected-exec mocking TOWARD a purpose-built fake of the domain interface

**Evidence**: Kubernetes historically took a `utilexec.Interface` parameter in the iptables/ipvs proxier constructors *"so you could pass a FakeExec to mock its behavior in unit tests."* That argument has now been removed. The stated rationale: the project has *"a fake implementation now that is **much more useful for unit tests than trying to use the real implementation with a fake exec**."* Unit tests still use fake execs internally, but *"they no longer need access to the public constructor that accepted the exec argument."* The `FakeExec` machinery itself is a first-class, long-lived public utility at [`kubernetes/utils/exec/testing/fake_exec.go`](https://github.com/kubernetes/utils/blob/master/exec/testing/fake_exec.go).
**Source**: [kubernetes/kubernetes PR #130561, "more misc proxy cleanup" (danwinship)](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/pull/130561) — Domain: `github.com` (trusted, industry_leaders, 0.8) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: The existence and long life of [`k8s.io/utils/exec`](https://github.com/kubernetes/utils/blob/master/exec/testing/fake_exec.go) (`github.com`, 0.8) independently establishes that injecting an interface over subprocess execution is a mainstream, decade-scale practice at Kubernetes; [`kubernetes-sigs/iptables-wrappers`](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/iptables-wrappers/) evidences the same team's treatment of the iptables surface as an explicitly abstracted, wrapped dependency.
**Confidence**: Medium-High (2 sources, same organisation — partial independence only; flagged)
**Analysis (interpretation) — this is the single most transferable precedent, and it is a *refinement*, not a rejection.** The `nft` / `ip` shell-outs in `mtls_intercept` are structurally identical to Kubernetes' `iptables` shell-outs. Kubernetes' arc is instructive in a way that directly grades our candidates:
- They **did** extract an interface over the un-ownable surface (validating candidate **A** in principle).
- They then **moved the abstraction to the domain level** — faking *"iptables"* rather than faking *"exec"* — because a fake of the low-level mechanism forces every test to encode command strings and exit codes, whereas a fake of the domain operation lets tests state intent.
For #250 this argues for a port whose methods are **domain-shaped** (`install_outbound_tproxy(...)`, `bind_transparent(...)`) rather than mechanism-shaped (`run_command(argv) -> ExitStatus`). That is exactly the shape candidate A proposes, and it is a concrete argument *against* a lower-level "process runner" port that someone will otherwise propose as more general.

#### Finding 6.2: Cilium takes the OPPOSITE approach for kernel/netlink work — it accepts "root required" and gates those tests out of the default run

**Evidence**: Cilium marks kernel-touching tests privileged and, per its contributor docs, *"They are skipped by default when running `go test`."* They are run explicitly via `sudo make tests-privileged`, optionally narrowed with `TESTPKGS`, or via `PRIVILEGED_TESTS=true go test -exec "sudo -E" ./pkg/ipam` — the docs noting this last form *"only escalates privileges when executing the test binaries, the `go build` process is run unprivileged."* Source files carry a `//go:build privileged_tests` build tag (e.g. `pkg/datapath/link/link_test.go`).
**Source**: [Cilium contributor docs, "Integration Testing" (unit)](https://docs.cilium.io/en/stable/contributing/testing/unit/) — Domain: `docs.cilium.io` (trusted, open_source, 1.0) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: [Cilium `pkg/datapath/link/link_test.go` with the `privileged_tests` build tag](https://fossies.org/linux/cilium/pkg/datapath/link/link_test.go) and [cilium/cilium issue #18861 on privileged_tests failures](https://github.com/cilium/cilium/issues/18861) (`github.com`, 0.8) both corroborate the mechanism in practice.
**Confidence**: High (3 sources)
**Analysis (interpretation) — this is the honest counter-precedent and it must not be buried.** Cilium is the closest peer to this codebase (eBPF, netlink, datapath, Linux-only) and it **does not** extract a fake-able port for its kernel surface; it segregates privileged tests behind a build tag and runs them with `sudo`. Read strictly, that is a vote for "accept that this is Tier-3-only." **But note precisely what it does and does not license.** Cilium's approach makes the *happy path* testable under root. It does **not** give them a way to make `bind_transparent` return `EPERM` on demand — the very thing #250 needs. Running the real thing as root produces *success*, not the scripted *failure* required to exercise a fail-closed handler. So the Cilium precedent is genuine evidence that "no port, integration-lane only" is a defensible industry position for *exercising* privileged code — while being **no evidence at all** that it can *fault-inject* privileged code. For #250's actual requirement (deterministic failure, default lane), the Cilium model does not reach.

#### Finding 6.3: The Rust ecosystem has no settled answer for faking netlink/privileged surfaces; trait-based injection is the default recommendation

**Evidence**: A Rust community help thread about the `rtnetlink` crate records that *"unit testing interactions with this crate is challenging, as it's not clear how to mock interactions with this dependency."* The ecosystem's general recommendation is trait-based: `mockall` *"provides tools to create mock versions of almost any trait"* via `#[automock]`, i.e. the mockable unit in Rust is **a trait**, not a free function.
**Source**: [The Rust Programming Language Forum, "Is it possible to mock this crate for unit testing?"](https://users.rust-lang.org/t/is-it-possible-to-mock-this-crate-for-unit-testing/107341) — Domain: `users.rust-lang.org` (subdomain of trusted `rust-lang.org`; community content → treated as **medium**, 0.6, cross-referenced) — Accessed 2026-08-01.
**Verification**: [`mockall` docs](https://docs.rs/mockall) (`docs.rs`, trusted, 1.0) confirms the trait-centric mocking model; [Rust Reference — Traits](https://doc.rust-lang.org/reference/items/traits.html) (1.0) confirms the language-level mechanism.
**Confidence**: Medium (the "no settled answer" claim rests on one community source; the trait-centric mechanism claim is High)
**Analysis (interpretation)**: The load-bearing mechanical fact — **in Rust, the substitutable unit is a trait** — is well established. Free functions are not substitutable without either a trait, a generic, a function pointer, or `cfg` swapping (RQ4.3). This means "make the free functions injectable" is not a design *option* separate from "extract a trait"; in Rust it is very nearly the *same* decision. That narrows the real choice for #250 to **where** the trait boundary sits (A vs B), not **whether** there is one.

## Knowledge Gaps

### Gap G1: The mutation-testing literature does not address production-code seams added for killability
**Issue**: No source found that directly evaluates "should you change production code to make a productive mutant killable?" The field's framing holds the program fixed and varies the test suite (see Finding 3.3).
**Attempted**: Searched ACM DL, arXiv, IEEE for mutation testing + testability refactoring + design-for-testability; reviewed Jia & Harman survey scope, both Google papers, the Facebook mutation-testing study. All treat mutants as *test-suite* feedback.
**Recommendation**: Treat this as genuinely out of the literature's scope. The decision must be argued on design (RQ1/RQ2) and security (RQ5) grounds, using the mutation result only as *evidence of an unasserted behaviour*. **Do not let a DESIGN artifact claim "the mutation-testing literature supports adding this seam."** It does not say so either way.

**Issue**: `dl.acm.org` returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch. Only abstract/metadata was obtained, so the *strength and direction* of the mutation-score↔real-fault correlation **after controlling for test suite size** is not established by this document.
**Attempted**: Direct ACM PDF (403), search-result summaries, adjacent citing papers.
**Recommendation**: If DESIGN wants to lean on aggregate mutation score as a gate metric, obtain this paper through institutional access first. **For #250 specifically this gap is not load-bearing** — the argument rests on one individual actionable mutant, not on an aggregate score (see Finding 3.4 analysis).

**Recommendation**: See Open Question OQ-2. This is a signature decision and must be pinned by the architect, not chosen by a crafter.

## Conflicting Information

### Conflict 1: Is indirection-for-testability a design improvement or design damage?
- **Position A (damage)**: DHH — code *"warped out of shape solely to accommodate testing objectives"* is damage; the test is *"If you did not look or care about the tests, you wouldn't look at the 'after code' and say 'BETTER!'"* Prefer integration/system tests over isolating indirection. Source: [dhh.dk](https://dhh.dk/2014/test-induced-design-damage.html), reputation 0.6 (primary-for-position).
- **Position B (not causal)**: Beck — the problem is *"one design decision at a time,"* not TDD; blaming the method is *"driving a car to a bad place and blaming the car for it."* Fowler — the driver is *"a desire for isolation,"* not TDD; decide by explicit trade-off (*"How much are we willing to spend to get how much decoupling?"*). Source: [martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/is-tdd-dead/), reputation 0.8.
- **Assessment**: **Position B is better supported and more authoritative** (higher-reputation source, two named authorities, and it survives the specific case). Crucially, the two positions *converge* on the same test for #250: DHH asks "is it better without the tests?" and Fowler/Beck ask "would a test fail if I broke this line?" — for a security fail-closed handler with a proven-missed mutant, **both** answers point the same way. This conflict is therefore **not decisive** for #250, though it would be for a lower-stakes handler.

### Conflict 2: Should privileged kernel surfaces be ported/faked, or segregated behind root-only tests?
- **Position A (extract and fake)**: Kubernetes removed injected `exec` in favour of a **dedicated domain-level fake**, because it is *"much more useful for unit tests than trying to use the real implementation with a fake exec."* Source: [k8s PR #130561](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/pull/130561), 0.8.
- **Position B (segregate, don't fake)**: Cilium marks kernel/netlink tests `//go:build privileged_tests`; they are *"skipped by default when running `go test`"* and run under `sudo`. No fake of the netlink surface. Source: [docs.cilium.io](https://docs.cilium.io/en/stable/contributing/testing/unit/), 1.0.
- **Assessment**: Both are real, mature practice; the conflict is **genuine, not an artefact**. It resolves on *what the test needs to do*: Cilium's model exercises privileged code paths **for success**; it provides no mechanism to make a privileged call **fail on demand**. #250 requires deterministic *failure* in the *default lane*. Position B cannot deliver that; Position A can. **Resolution: not actually contradictory once the requirement is stated precisely** — Cilium's approach and a port are complements (this repo already does both: real Tier-3 tests *and* `Sim*` adapters), not alternatives.

## Recommendation

> **Ranked verdict for THIS codebase. The mechanical findings are evidence-backed; the ranking and the reconciliation against repo rules are analysis and are labelled as such.**

### Rank 1 — **Candidate A: extract an `MtlsIntercept` port trait, injected as a 4th mandatory `Arc<dyn …>` on `MtlsInterceptWorker::new`**

**Why it wins, against each repo rule the prompt named:**

| Repo rule | How A satisfies it |
|---|---|
| *"Production code is not shaped by simulation"* | **Satisfied — and this is the decisive discriminator.** A adds **no** test-shaped construct to production: no fault field, no `cfg`, no `Option`, no comment explaining a sim quirk. Production gains one more `Arc<dyn Port>` that is *indistinguishable in shape* from the three the worker already holds. The rule's own symptom list (a `_dst_yield_hook` param, a `// sim:` comment, a "redundant but harmless" docstring) contains **nothing** that A introduces. By contrast, candidate C trips the rule's symptom list directly. |
| *"Required, not defaulted, at the call site"* | **Satisfied by construction** — a 4th mandatory `new()` parameter is exactly the rule's prescribed shape. |
| *"Builder-pattern overrides are an anti-pattern"* | **Satisfied** — no `with_intercept()`. Compare: candidates C and the fn-pointer-field variant are *definitionally* optional seams, i.e. the anti-pattern. |
| *"Trait definitions specify behavior, not just signature"* | **Satisfied, at a cost** — A obliges a full rustdoc contract (preconditions / postconditions / edge cases / observable invariants) **plus** a DST equivalence test across host and sim adapters. This is real work and must be budgeted, not hand-waved. It is also the rule that makes A *more* than a testing hack: the contract is the artifact that makes the boundary legitimate under DHH's own criterion. |
| *"Never put `overdrive-sim` in `[dependencies]`"* | **Satisfied** — `SimMtlsIntercept` lives in `overdrive-sim`, dev-dep only, matching `SimMtlsResolve`. |
| `.claude/rules/testing.md` default lane | **Satisfied** — a sim adapter is pure in-process, no I/O, no subprocess, no sockets, sub-second. This is the *only* candidate that delivers #250's stated default-lane requirement. |

**Supporting evidence**: Finding 1.3 (the canonical remedy for un-ownable APIs is exactly this wrapper); Finding 6.1 (Kubernetes converged on a *domain-level* fake for the structurally identical `iptables` surface); Finding 1.2 (test-drivability is original-intent for ports); Finding 5.3 (the syscall boundary is a published, principled injection point); Finding 6.3 (in Rust the substitutable unit *is* a trait, so "make the free fns injectable" and "extract a trait" are near-identical decisions).

**Shape constraints that fall out of the research** (inputs to DESIGN, not decisions):
- Methods must be **domain-shaped**, not mechanism-shaped — `install_outbound_tproxy(...)`, not `run_command(argv)`. (Finding 6.1: this is precisely the refinement Kubernetes made.)
- Trait must be **dyn compatible**: no generic methods, no associated consts, `&self` receiver, no RPITIT. (Finding 4.2.)
- Per repo convention the port likely needs an **Earned-Trust `probe()`** (`wire → probe → use`, as `MtlsResolve` has). Bonus, not cost: a probe would surface a missing `CAP_NET_ADMIN` at boot instead of at first allocation.
- The sim adapter should script **realistic** errors (`EPERM`, `nft` non-zero exit, `EADDRINUSE`) rather than a generic boolean — Finding 5.3's realism criterion, and it matches `SimMtlsResolve::script_resolve_fault` / `SimObservationStore::inject_write_failure`.

### Rank 2 — **Candidate D-variant: A, but scoped to the smallest boundary that makes the mutant killable**

Not a different mechanism — a **sizing discipline** on A, and the honest response to Fowler's *"How much are we willing to spend to get how much decoupling?"* (Finding 2.2) and to Cockburn's "number of ports is a matter of taste, no damage either way" (Finding 1.1). If DESIGN judges a 3-method port over-scoped, a **single-method** port (`install(&self, spec) -> Result<InstalledLegs, MtlsInterceptInstallError>`) wrapping the whole `start_alloc` install sequence still kills the mutant, still satisfies every repo rule above, and is a materially smaller contract surface to specify and equivalence-test. **Ranked second only because it may under-serve future needs** (it cannot fault-inject *which* of the three steps failed, and #250's sibling scenarios may want that). DESIGN should choose between Rank 1 and Rank 2 explicitly.

### Rank 3 — **Candidate B: make the `action_shim` boundary itself a port**

**Rejected as the primary, for a specific and checkable reason.** Moving the abstraction up to `Option<&Arc<dyn SomeInterceptPort>>` at the action-shim would let a test inject a failing worker — but it abstracts **the wrong thing**. The un-ownable surface (per Finding 1.3) is `libc`/`nft`, not `MtlsInterceptWorker`; `MtlsInterceptWorker` is code we own, and mocking it means the test no longer exercises the worker's real logic. It also widens the blast radius (every `action_shim` call site and the composition root change) for a *smaller* testability gain, which is the trade Fowler's criterion (3) counsels against. It is, additionally, the closest of all four candidates to what DHH actually calls damage: hollowing out a concrete collaborator into an interface purely so a test can substitute it. **Keep B available as a fallback** if DESIGN finds A structurally blocked, but it should not be the default.

### Rank 4 — **Candidate C: `#[cfg(feature = "integration-tests")]` fault field**

**Rejected, on three independent grounds, any one of which is sufficient:**
1. **It cannot meet the stated requirement.** #250 wants a **default-lane** killer test. The default lane compiles *without* `integration-tests`. A feature-gated seam is therefore invisible to the very test it exists to enable. (Finding 4.1a/4.1b.)
2. **It is the anti-builder rule in another costume.** An `Option<Fault>` field defaulted to `None` is an optional dependency — *"'optional' means 'tests can forget.'"*
3. **It is production code shaped by simulation, by definition.** A field that exists only to make a test double work is the exact symptom the rule enumerates. Cargo's own additivity guidance (*"enabling a feature should not disable functionality"*) flags a behaviour-changing fault knob as the smell it is; feature unification (Finding 4.1b) means it compiles into builds that also produce production artifacts.
The existing `ControlPlaneConfig::mtls_probe_fault` precedent does **not** rescue C: those are *integration-lane* seams for scenarios that were never claimed to be default-lane, so they are not precedent for the case at hand.

### The strongest counter-argument to this recommendation

**It is this, and it should be taken seriously rather than dismissed:**

> *A port is being extracted whose only present consumer-visible benefit is a test. The `MtlsIntercept` boundary has exactly one production implementation and no plausible second one — there will never be a "different way to install TPROXY." Cockburn himself says the number of ports is a matter of taste with no damage either way (Finding 1.1), and DHH's criterion (Finding 2.1) bites hard: strip the tests away and a reader sees three free functions replaced by a trait, an adapter, a `dyn` indirection, a mandatory constructor parameter threaded to the composition root, a full four-section rustdoc contract, and a DST equivalence test — for zero runtime behaviour change. Meanwhile Cilium — the closest peer project in the world to this codebase — looked at exactly this problem and chose NOT to abstract the kernel surface (Finding 6.2). That is a serious industry vote for "accept Tier-3-only," and this recommendation is overriding it.*

**Why the recommendation nonetheless stands** (and this is the part DESIGN should stress-test):
- Cilium's precedent proves privileged code can be *exercised* under root; it provides **no** mechanism to make a privileged call *fail on demand* (Conflict 2). It therefore does not actually address #250's requirement, and citing it as if it did would be the error.
- The "only one implementation" objection is materially weakened by the fact that **there is already a second one** the moment a sim adapter exists — which is precisely the situation with `MtlsResolve`, `MtlsEnforcement`, `Clock`, `Driver`, and `Dataplane`, none of which are considered over-abstraction in this repo. A is *consistent with*, not an exception to, the codebase's established shape.
- The security asymmetry (Finding 5.2) is the tiebreaker: this is a fail-closed control whose silent failure *"may go unnoticed in normal use,"* on a path where Yuan et al. found the majority of catastrophic failures live (Finding 5.1). That justifies a cost that an ordinary error path would not.

**If DESIGN finds the counter-argument persuasive**, the honest alternative is **not** candidate C — it is to **accept the gap explicitly**: keep the `exclude_re` suppression, but reclassify it in the config from "REMOVE when #250 lands" to a permanent, justified exclusion citing the Cilium precedent, and add a Tier-3 test that exercises the *success* path. That is a defensible engineering position. What is **not** defensible is leaving a suppression labelled temporary indefinitely, because per Finding 3.2 it is suppressing a *productive* mutant, and Google's own taxonomy does not sanction that.

## Open Questions for DESIGN

Per this repo's *"Implement to the design — never invent API surface"* rule, the following are **unresolved by this research** and must be pinned by the architect before any crafter dispatch. A crafter encountering any of these should return a blocker, not choose.

- **OQ-1 — Port granularity: 3 methods or 1?** Rank 1 (`bind_transparent` / `install_outbound` / `install_inbound`) vs Rank 2 (single `install(...)`). Research finds no external criterion (Finding 1.1); this is a deliberate local judgement about future fault-injection granularity.
- **OQ-2 — Sync or `#[async_trait]`?** The repo's stated criterion on `MtlsResolve` is *"async only where the contract genuinely awaits store I/O."* Blocking `libc::socket`/`setsockopt` argues sync; a `tokio::process`-spawned `nft` argues async. Sync is dyn-compatible with no `Box<dyn Future>` allocation per call (Finding 4.2). **Pin the exact signature.** (Gap G5.)
- **OQ-3 — Which crate owns the trait?** `MtlsResolve` lives in `overdrive-core/src/traits/`. A port over `libc`/`nft` is Linux-privileged I/O; whether it belongs in `core` (trait only, no I/O — permissible) or elsewhere interacts with dst-lint and ADR-0003 crate classes.
- **OQ-4 — Does the port carry an Earned-Trust `probe()`?** `MtlsResolve` does (`wire → probe → use`, ADR-0071). If yes, define what a `CAP_NET_ADMIN` probe asserts and whether probe failure is boot-refusing.
- **OQ-5 — Exact `MtlsInterceptInstallError` construction escape.** Confirm the real blocker (per-variant `#[non_exhaustive]` vs private field types vs struct-expression variants) and pin the sanctioned escape — public constructor fn (repo-preferred), `From` bridge, or `#[doc(hidden)]` (weakest). (Finding 4.4, Gap G4.)
- **OQ-6 — Duplicate call sites.** `fail_closed_on_mtls_install` is invoked from two byte-identical blocks (`StartAllocation` `mod.rs:1307`, `RestartAllocation` `mod.rs:1507`). Does the killer test cover both arms, or does DESIGN also collapse the duplication? (Two arms = two mutants.)
- **OQ-7 — Assertion surface for the killer test.** Which observable does the test assert on — the superseding `Failed` `AllocStatusRow`, the `TransitionReason::MtlsInterceptInstallFailed`, the lifecycle event, the driver-stop call, the *non*-release of the exit-emission gate, or all five? Per Finding 3.2, the assertion must pin the **specification**, not the implementation, or it becomes the brittle change-detector Google names as unproductive. **The deliberate non-release of the gate is the subtlest and most easily-omitted assertion; it is also a distinct mutation target.**
- **OQ-8 — Scope of the DST equivalence test.** The repo rule requires host↔sim observable equivalence for port traits. For a port whose host adapter needs `CAP_NET_ADMIN`, the equivalence test cannot run in the default lane. Define what is asserted where (default-lane sim-only contract test + Tier-3 host conformance?).
- **OQ-9 — Suppression removal contract.** Confirm that landing #250 removes the `.cargo/mutants.toml` `exclude_re` entry in the *same* commit, and that the mutation gate is re-run to prove the mutant is now caught. Per Finding 3.2 the entry is currently suppressing a productive mutant.
